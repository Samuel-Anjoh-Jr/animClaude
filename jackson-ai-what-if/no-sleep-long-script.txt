No sleep. Not tonight. Not this week. Never.

Midnight — everyone else is winding down, and you're just getting started.
Two a.m. — home-run level 23, and nothing even blurs.
Four a.m. — you finish the book. Start a second one.
The first week awake feels like a superpower. Everyone swears you look fine.

Here's the catch: sleep isn't a pause. It's a cleaning crew.
While you dream, channels in your brain open up, and wash the waste out — plaque, toxins, the debris linked to Alzheimer's. No sleep, no rinse.

And it shows fast. Seventeen hours awake, and you drive like someone at 0.05 blood alcohol. Twenty-four hours — 0.10. Above every legal limit on Earth.

Day two: your immune system measurably weakens. One week of short sleep around a vaccine, and your body makes only half the antibodies it should.

Day three: the brain starts micro-sleeping. Two-second blackouts. Waking dreams. It will not obey "awake."

A student once survived eleven days awake, doctors tracking him the whole way. But in the Chicago sleep labs, rats kept awake didn't fade gently. Their immune systems collapsed. Their skin broke down. Weeks — and they were gone.

Sleep isn't a habit. It's plumbing. You can skip the wash, but the pipes keep filling.

So — how long could you last?
